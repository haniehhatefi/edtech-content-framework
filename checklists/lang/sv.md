# Swedish Language Module
**Family:** Germanic | **Code:** `sv`

Complete the [universal checklist](../universal.md) first.

---

## 1 — The `du`-Reform and Register

Swedish underwent a fundamental social shift in the 1960s–70s known as the *du-reformen* — the deliberate and near-total elimination of formal address forms. Today, Swedish is almost universally informal in address across consumer, educational, and most professional contexts.

- [ ] Use `du` for addressing the learner. For consumer EdTech, this is the correct default across virtually all Swedish audiences.
- [ ] Do not use *Ni* (formal plural/formal singular) for addressing an individual learner. *Ni* is archaic for second-person singular address and reads as either ironic or very old-fashioned in everyday modern Swedish. *(Exception: a small number of very formal governmental or legal texts may retain *Ni* — this is not relevant for a consumer EdTech platform.)*
- [ ] Do not translate English formal register (*you* → *Ni*) — use *du*.
- [ ] In practice, this makes Swedish the most straightforward register decision in this framework: *du* is correct in almost every consumer or educational context.

## 2 — Tone: Calm, Flat, and Matter-of-Fact

Swedish communication in professional and educational contexts often favors a calm, low-key, non-hierarchical tone. This tendency is sometimes described in relation to the *Jantelagen* cultural concept: avoid appearing superior, avoid excessive praise of individuals. Note that *Jantelagen* is a literary/cultural shorthand, not a universal rule — younger audiences and informal contexts vary. Use the checklist items below as practical defaults, not absolute law.

- [ ] Enthusiastic feedback (*Du är fantastisk! Otroligt bra jobbat!*) is toned down significantly. It reads as exaggerated or patronizing.
- [ ] Correct answer feedback is simple and factual: *Rätt svar.* or *Korrekt.* — not *Jättebra! Du klarade det!*
- [ ] Incorrect answer feedback is calm and informative: *Det stämmer inte. Nämnaren är det undre talet.* — direct but not harsh.
- [ ] Hierarchy is flat in Swedish educational culture. Do not write content that positions the platform as an authority figure rewarding or judging the learner — frame it as a tool that supports learning.
- [ ] Avoid content that singles out individual achievement in a competitive framing. Progress is presented in terms of the learner's own development, not comparison with others.

## 3 — Grammatical Gender

Modern Swedish has simplified grammatical gender to two: common (*en*-words) and neuter (*ett*-words). There is no masculine/feminine split.

- [ ] Nouns use the correct article (*en* or *ett*). This is not fully predictable — verify with a native speaker.
- [ ] Generic references to learners: *eleven* (pupil, school context), *studenten* (university), *användaren* (user) — all common gender (*en*). Safe for gender-neutral use.
- [ ] Swedish has no grammatically gendered pronouns for humans in standard use: *hen* (gender-neutral) was officially added to Swedish dictionaries in 2015 and is widely accepted. *Han* (he) and *hon* (she) exist but should only be used when gender is known.
- [ ] In generic educational content, *hen* or *användaren/eleven/studenten* is the correct approach.

---

## 4 — Swedish Cultural Assumptions

- [ ] References to Swedish institutions, geography, or culture are accurate and current.
- [ ] Examples involving social structures reflect Swedish norms: flat hierarchies, collaborative decision-making, welfare state framing (education is a right, not a privilege).
- [ ] Content about failure and improvement does not frame learning as competition. Swedish educational philosophy emphasizes mastery and process over ranking.

---

## 5 — Typography and Formatting

- [ ] Swedish letters *å*, *ä*, *ö* are correct and not substituted with *a* or *o*. These are distinct letters in the Swedish alphabet.
- [ ] Quotation marks: "så här" (standard) or »så här« (also used). Choose one convention.
- [ ] Decimal separator: comma (*3,14*). Thousand separator: space (*1 000*).
- [ ] Dates: YYYY-MM-DD (ISO format) is standard in formal Swedish; DD/MM/YYYY also used informally.
- [ ] `maxLength` in UI labels: Swedish strings are typically close to English in length — allow 10–15% expansion as a margin.
